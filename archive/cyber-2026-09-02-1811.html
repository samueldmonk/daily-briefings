Top Story

SonicWall SMA 1000 appliances are under attack through two zero-days that chain into unauthenticated RCE

SonicWall confirmed in advisory SNWLID-2026-0016 that attackers are exploiting two previously undisclosed vulnerabilities in its SMA 1000 secure remote-access appliances — SSL VPN gateways sold to medium and large enterprises, government agencies and managed security service providers.

CVE-2026-83548 is a pre-authentication server-side request forgery flaw in the Appliance Work Place interface that lets remote unauthenticated attackers, in the vendor’s words, gain unauthorized access to sensitive functionality and perform unauthorized operations. Security press reporting this run gives it a maximum CVSS v3 score of 10.0, attributed by SonicWall to an unintended alternative access path. CVE-2026-83549 is an OS command injection flaw in the Appliance Management Console, scored 7.8 by the same reporting, which under specific conditions lets an authenticated administrator execute arbitrary OS commands. Reporting this run states the two can be chained to achieve unauthenticated remote code execution on unpatched appliances, and that attackers are likely doing exactly that.

Affected models are the physical and virtual 6210, 7210 and 8200v. SMA 100 appliances and SonicWall firewalls are not affected. Hotfixes 12.4.3-03526 and 12.5.0-02952 and later address the flaws; affected builds are 12.4.3-03453 / 12.5.0-02835 and older. SonicWall’s own William Perry and Adam Babis are credited with the discovery.

SonicWall says its product security incident response team investigated a case indicating active exploitation and “strongly urged” customers to apply the hotfix. Customers are told to contact support to review appliances for indicators of compromise, and on confirmed compromise to re-image hardware or re-deploy virtual appliances, change every user and administrator password, and reset TOTP tokens. No public indicators of compromise have been shared, and no further detail about the attacks has been released.

CISA added CVE-2026-83549 to the Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalog on September 2 with a remediation due date of September 5. This is the third distinct wave of SMA 1000 zero-day exploitation this desk can source, after June–July 2026 and late 2025.

On the two severity scores. The vendor advisory page was not read directly this run; the 10.0 and the 7.8 come from security-press reporting of it. This desk has twice published an inflated score taken from a blog — Citrix’s official figure was 9.3 against a reported 9.8, and Progress’s was 9.6 — so both numbers here are attributed rather than adopted. Nothing about the chain depends on either score.

A dating discrepancy, printed rather than resolved. Help Net Security states the vendor confirmed the flaws “on Tuesday” — September 1 — while the KEV addition is dated September 2. Both are printed; the KEV date is the one the deadline runs from.

Patch Priority

Patch first — SonicWall SMA 1000

CVE-2026-83548 (CVSS 10.0) and CVE-2026-83549 (CVSS 7.8), SonicWall SMA 1000 models 6210 / 7210 / 8200v. Apply hotfix 12.4.3-03526 or 12.5.0-02952 or later. This outranks everything else on the page on the combination that matters: a maximum-severity pre-authentication flaw, on an internet-facing appliance, confirmed by the vendor as exploited, chainable to unauthenticated code execution, and carrying a federal deadline of Saturday, September 5. That deadline is attached in KEV to CVE-2026-83549 specifically; the hotfix closes both, so the practical unit of work is the appliance, not the CVE.

The one item with a shorter clock is MLflow’s CVE-2026-64849, whose KEV deadline expires today. It is not ranked first here only because it is a self-hosted machine-learning platform rather than an edge VPN, and because a deadline that has already arrived offers nothing left to schedule. If MLflow is unpatched in your estate, it is already late — treat both as today’s work.

Threat Actor Spotlight

ShinyHunters — extortion, no encryption

McKesson: a terabyte out the door in four days, and a record count that needs reading carefully

McKesson confirmed unauthorized access to third-party applications and data theft after discovering the incident on August 25. ShinyHunters says it gained entry through vishing calls to McKesson employees, used the stolen credentials to take over Okta single sign-on accounts, then moved into the company’s Salesforce and Snowflake environments and pulled out about a terabyte of data over four days. The group is reported to have demanded roughly $55 million.

Data said to be involved includes personally identifiable and protected health information, medical and treatment records, prescription and billing records, employee records, and information on McKesson’s customer physicians and clinics — with names, addresses, Social Security numbers, diagnoses and medications among the fields named.

Correction to this desk’s earlier editions. The widely quoted figure of 284 million is not a count of unique individuals. ShinyHunters has clarified that it is a raw count of roughly 284 million data records, or lines. Earlier editions of this page printed it as “284 million patient records” without that qualification.

Breaches & Incidents

Aesto Health — healthcare technology

9.5 million people affected

NewPHI + SSN

More than 9.5 million people had personal and health information stolen from healthcare technology company Aesto Health. The company’s investigation determined on May 26, 2026 that attackers exfiltrated data between December 2 and 18, reaching the company’s AWS infrastructure. Fields include names, Social Security numbers, driver’s licence and other ID numbers, dates of birth, financial account numbers, medical and health-insurance information and taxpayer identification numbers. At least two dozen provider clients across several states are affected.
