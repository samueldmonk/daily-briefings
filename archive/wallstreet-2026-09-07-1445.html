The Lead

Labor Day: no session — and CENTCOM confirms it struck three Iranian tankers as Hormuz traffic hits a four-month low

Markets are closed. Today is Monday 7 September, Labor Day: the New York Stock Exchange and Nasdaq are shut all day, and the U.S. bond market is closed too per SIFMA. The NYSE reopens at normal hours, 9:30 AM ET on Tuesday 8 September. Newly sourced the 12:16 edition, and it matters to anyone watching a futures screen this afternoon rather than an equity one: the derivatives market is not open all day either. CME Group’s equity index futures halt at 12:00 p.m. Central — 1:00 PM ET — and move to a pre-open state in which order entry, modification and cancellation are permitted but no orders are matched; they reopen at 5:00 p.m. CT. CME also says it will publish no Monday settlement prices for CME, CBOT, NYMEX or COMEX products, and that day orders entered after the Sunday pre-open are for trade date Tuesday 8 September. That halt has now happened. The 12:16 edition said the live ticker at the top of this page had roughly two hours of matched trading left in it; as the 2:15 edition publishes at about 2:15 PM ET, the 1:00 PM ET halt is about an hour and a quarter in the past, so the equity-index quotes in the tape above are the last matched prices of a shortened holiday session, not a live market — a quiet screen, which is not the same thing as a flat one. Two returns in the 11:17 edition agree on the 12:00 CT halt; both also carry the standard warning to check the exchange and broker schedule directly, and this desk repeats it rather than presenting a relayed time as authoritative. Everything below on equities reflects Friday 4 September, the most recent completed session. Refreshed the 12:16 edition: a Trading Economics commodity return dated today has Brent rising toward $97 a barrel on Monday, extending last week’s gains as the United States and Iran exchange strikes, and adds Tehran’s answer to Saturday’s tanker strikes — attacks on tankers and other vessels linked to the United States, and a “restricted” maritime zone beyond the Strait of Hormuz said to be coming in the next few days. “Toward $97” is the direction and the neighbourhood as the return states them; this desk still declines to assert a single Brent print as the live level, for the twenty-seventh consecutive edition, because no timestamped quote was reached. Oil trades while the NYSE is dark, and over the weekend the U.S.–Iran confrontation moved from missiles to tankers: Brent has run to a six-week high, its highest since 24 July, a date sourced in the 9:17 edition from EnergyNow quoting Reuters, and — per Trading Economics, fetched directly in the 8:47 edition — nearly 40% above levels from before the outbreak of the war in Iran. Four things were newly sourced in the 9:17 edition, all from EnergyNow’s 7 September market report, fetched directly. U.S. Central Command is now the named source for the strikes — it says American forces hit three Iranian oil tankers on Saturday, one of them near Kharg Island, the centre of Iran’s crude-export system. The physical effect is measurable: Kpler counts an average of only ten commodity ships a day transiting Hormuz over the past ten days, the lowest level since May. The range of outcomes has been priced by a bank willing to put its name to it: Goldman Sachs says crude could reach $120 a barrel if attacks on Middle Eastern shipping intensify, while a normalisation of regional exports could pull it back toward $80. And the producer group offered no relief — OPEC+ left its October output policy unchanged over the weekend while members work toward new quotas. The Oman thread is not new — this page has carried “Iran says it is close to agreeing a tanker route through Hormuz with Oman” since the 1:45 edition. What is new the 2:15 edition is that it now has specifics and, more to the point, a price reaction attached to it, and the reaction corrects the shape of the day rather than adding to it. Bloomberg, in a report dated today, says the two countries have been negotiating for weeks, that the agreement is in its final stages and will set out a temporary safe route through the waterway, and that it would be documented with the International Maritime Organization, the United Nations shipping body. The talks also covered technical arrangements, the security and sovereignty of Iran and Oman, and a proposed joint coordination centre to manage maritime traffic and collect information from vessels. The caveat is Tehran’s own: Iran says the United States must meet further conditions before vessels can transit freely, and Bloomberg’s own framing is that Washington is unlikely to accept Iran having a say over which ships may pass.
