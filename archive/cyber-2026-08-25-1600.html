Top story

A phishing kit is letting victims finish two-factor authentication — then stealing the session it produces

Mirage2FA is a commercial phishing-as-a-service platform built specifically to defeat multi-factor authentication on corporate Microsoft 365 accounts, and The Hacker News reported today on research putting its reach at roughly 4,500 companies across the United States and the European Union. The mechanism is an adversary-in-the-middle reverse proxy. The victim lands on a convincing fake Microsoft login page and types a username, a password and a one-time 2FA code; the kit relays all three to the genuine Microsoft service in real time over a WebSocket channel, the login succeeds, and Mirage2FA keeps both the credentials and the valid authenticated session cookie that comes back. Nothing about the login looks wrong to the user, and nothing about it looks wrong to the second factor either.

The scale numbers come from ANY.RUN telemetry and are worth reading carefully. Of 9,426 unique targeted email addresses, 4,532 were potentially compromised — a success rate of about 48%. Activity has been recorded in 94 countries, with 63.7% of identified victims in the United States. ANY.RUN attributes the kit to an operator it calls LinX Coders and dates the campaign from September 2024 to July 2026; a separate Fortra write-up describes the same kit reaching victims through an obfuscated HTML loader. Delivery is a phishing email carrying an .htm, .xhtml or .svg attachment, or a QR-code link. Almost the entire attack runs in the browser. The two figures in circulation — “roughly 4,500 companies” in the headline reporting and 4,532 potentially compromised addresses in the telemetry — are printed here as they were stated, and this page does not treat them as the same count.

Why it belongs at the top of a defender’s reading list. Most MFA rollouts are scoped against credential theft, and this campaign is not credential theft in the sense that scoping assumes — the password and the second factor both work exactly as designed, and the attacker walks away with a cookie representing an already-authenticated session. Detection has to move to where the evidence actually is: impossible-travel and anomalous-session signals in sign-in logs, session-token binding, conditional-access policies that re-evaluate rather than trust a token to expiry, and phishing-resistant factors such as hardware security keys or passkeys, which do not survive a relay because they are bound to the origin. The attachment types are a usable control point too — .svg as an inbound email attachment has almost no legitimate business use.

Carried · 1:34 editionRansomwareMedusa

Medusa has passed 500 victims, and the federal advisory on it has been rewritten

The FBI, CISA and the Department of Health and Human Services updated their joint #StopRansomware advisory on Medusa on August 18, 2026, with data drawn from FBI investigations as of April 2026. The headline number moved sharply: Medusa developers and affiliates have now impacted more than 500 victims across critical infrastructure sectors, against the more than 300 the agencies reported in the March 2025 version of the same advisory. Affected industries named include medical, education, legal, insurance, technology and manufacturing, with healthcare, defence, government services, IT and financial services also listed.

The mechanic worth planning for is the clock. Medusa runs a double-extortion model, and the agencies describe a 48-hour window: victims get two days to respond to the ransom note before the group begins contacting them directly and posts the stolen data to a leak site with a countdown timer. That is an incident-response design constraint, not a trivia point — it means the decision-making chain has to be exercised before the note arrives, not after. The agencies’ recommendations are unglamorous and unchanged: patch internet-facing systems, segment networks to limit lateral movement, and block untrusted traffic from reaching remote-access services.

Group first observed in June 2021. The 500+ figure is the count as of April 2026, published August 18, 2026 — it is a cumulative victim total, not a current-month rate.

Carried · 3:08 editionCredential theftMicrosoft TeamsNo CVE

SynkLoader impersonates the IT help desk on Teams, then fakes a Windows lock screen

A previously unknown modular malware family called SynkLoader is being pushed through Microsoft Teams phishing, with the operator posing as the target company’s own IT help desk — a pretext Microsoft flagged earlier this year as increasingly common in multi-stage intrusions. The victim is talked into installing a fake “PowerShell Cleaner” MSI hosted in Microsoft Azure, which lends the download a trustworthy origin. Compile dates and file timestamps put first distribution around July 28, 2026. The installer drops a PowerShell script, cleaner.ps1, plus a ZIP containing a bundled Python framework, a malicious Python script, precompiled libraries and several fake Microsoft runtime DLLs. From there it is modular: PhishLocker throws a convincing full-screen Windows lock screen to capture the login password; a persistence module registers a randomly named scheduled task that fires at logon and again daily at 10 a.m.; TrafficRedirector stands up a reverse proxy into internal services; an interactive shell runs PowerShell remotely; and StreamMaster streams the desktop with mouse and keyboard control. A usable tell for end users: the fake lock screen is a borderless full-screen application, so Alt+Tab or Ctrl+Alt+Delete exposes it. No CVE and no CVSS attach to this — it is a social-engineering chain, not a product flaw, and neither is published here.

Carried · 3:08 editionCloud exposureAWSNo CVE

More than 9,300 leaked AWS access keys still authenticate — hundreds with full admin

Truffle Security says it found 64,024 unique AWS key pairs across 431,875 public findings — git history, Hugging Face datasets, Docker images, package registries and CI logs — surfacing between August 2022 and August 2026. Of the 10,616 pairs it was able to test, 88% still authenticate as of August 10, 2026, which is where the headline figure of more than 9,300 live keys comes from. The severity is in the tail rather than the total: 817 exposed keys were tied to identifiable companies, 526 were AWS root keys, and 242 belonged to IAM users carrying the AdministratorAccess policy — full permissions across the account’s services. The researchers say no key material was published and every owner they could identify is being notified. The defensive reading is unglamorous and specific: a credential that leaked in 2022 is not historical if it was never rotated, so the work here is an inventory-and-rotate pass against public sources rather than a patch cycle, with root keys deleted outright and IAM roles substituted for long-lived user keys.

Carried · 3:08 editionBanking trojanAndroidNo CVE

ToxicPanda 2.0 turns Android’s VPN permission against Google Play Protect

The ToxicPanda Android banking trojan has been rebuilt, and the upgrade is mostly about defence evasion. The new build requests VPN service permission through a fake installation screen, then uses that local interface to block communication from Google Play and Google Play Services — silencing Play Protect while it decrypts and installs the real payload hidden in the dropper’s own asset files. Zimperium, which published the research, says ToxicPanda 2.0 now supports 167 remote commands and phishing overlays for 349 banking, financial, cryptocurrency and e-wallet applications across 16 countries, plus a separate PIN-harvesting module aimed at 140 financial and crypto apps with a target list it can update on the fly. The overlays are invisible to the victim and capture touch input on the targeted app; the malware also spoofs the Android lock screen to lift device PINs, unlock patterns and passwords. Distribution is via AWS-hosted buckets. No CVE and no CVSS — this is a malicious application, not a platform vulnerability, and neither is published here.

Carried · 2:44 editionNovel techniqueTwo new RATsNo CVE

Attackers are hiding their commands in FTP login banners

A campaign back on the wire in today’s trade coverage is using FTP server banners — the greeting text a server sends before a client logs in — as dead-drop resolvers, embedding attacker commands in that greeting so a stager can pull instructions without ever touching a web service. SOCRadar, whose report BleepingComputer carried, says the technique has been weaponised since early July 2026 and remains operational, with new infrastructure observed as recently as August 2026. The chain starts with a ZIP archive that triggers an LNK-based infection; the researchers say initial compromise most likely arrives by phishing. MalwareHunterTeam first spotted it in July.

Two previously undocumented remote access trojans come out the other end. E4del is a Node.js RAT packaged inside a digitally signed Electron application that masquerades as Discord; it runs commands through persistent or temporary shells, captures screenshots, streams the desktop over WebSockets and downloads further payloads. SOCRadar also saw a Node module named crypto32.node attempting privilege escalation but could not retrieve it for analysis. PINHOLE pulls its command-and-control configuration from Pinterest pins and SurveyMonkey survey questions, keeps only a single 4KB section of its payload in memory at a time through shellcode fluctuation, and injects its final assembly into a suspended ApplicationFrameHost.exe by Early Bird APC injection. It supports 14 commands, including file enumeration, upload and download, process management, screenshots and a module for stealing browser-stored credentials.

Scale, and the one thing that makes it easier to catch. At the time of analysis the PINHOLE script counted only 11 execution events, which SOCRadar reads as an early-stage campaign. The researchers also name the trade-off the attackers have accepted: FTP banners are a novel channel, but they are less stealthy than the usual web-based dead drops on X, GitHub or YouTube, because outbound FTP connections to unfamiliar servers stand out on a network where web traffic does not. They warn the technique could “easily” be adapted to ClickFix-style social engineering, and have published indicators of compromise. No CVE and no CVSS attach to this — it is a delivery technique, not a product flaw — so neither is published. Dates disclosed: BleepingComputer’s write-up is stamped August 21, 07:00 a.m.; the story re-entered the wire through The Hacker News today.

Carried · 2:00 editionMass exploitationZimbra

274 Zimbra servers are already compromised, and about 8,200 remain unpatched

Shadowserver reported on Monday that it had spotted hundreds of internet-exposed Zimbra instances already breached through CVE-2026-73570. Its exact wording: “Zimbra compromises associated with CVE-2026-73570 exploitation are spreading. 274 instances seen compromised in our scans for exploitation artifacts on 2026-08-22.” It adds that it also sees at least 8,200 unpatched instances — while noting explicitly that unpatched does not mean exploitable, because the flaw sits in a non-default configuration.

The mechanism. Synacor patched the flaw — an OS command injection in the SNMP monitoring component, reachable when SNMP notifications are enabled, giving unauthenticated attackers remote code execution — in ZCS version 10.1.20, released July 20. CERT Polska first flagged it as targeted in the wild last Monday and told teams to check logs for the Zimbra service restarting unexpectedly and for files created by the zimbra user in /opt/zimbra/jetty/webapps/, /opt/zimbra/jetty_base/webapps/ and /tmp/ over the last 30 days. CISA added it to KEV on August 21 with a three-day federal deadline of August 24 — now lapsed.

ZCS is used by hundreds of millions of people, including thousands of businesses and hundreds of government agencies, and its flaws have a long history as state-actor targets: Russia’s APT28 was seen abusing a stored XSS bug against Ukrainian government servers in March, US and UK agencies warned in October 2024 that APT29 had compromised Zimbra servers, and Winter Vivern used a reflected XSS flaw to steal mail from NATO-aligned accounts.

Carried · 1:34 editionWordPressExploited

miniOrange SAML SSO for WordPress is under active exploitation

Attackers are chaining two critical authentication-bypass flaws in the miniOrange SAML 2.0 Single Sign On plugin to forge SAML responses and log in as administrators. CVE-2026-61979 is a signature-algorithm confusion issue: the plugin honours the algorithm named in the incoming SAML response, so an attacker selects HMAC-SHA1 and the plugin treats the identity provider’s public RSA key as a shared secret. CVE-2026-15981 is the second half: mo_saml_validate_signature() loose-checks the tri-state integer returned by openssl_verify(), so the −1 error value reads as success and malformed signatures validate.

Vendor Xecurify ships seven editions; the free version has about 10,000 downloads and the other six about 30,000 customers. The flaws were disclosed and fixed in July, but the vendor advisory covered only the free edition, so the six paid editions received no alert and WordPress shows their users no update warning. Patched builds: 5.4.5 / 13.0.4 / 17.06 / 20.2.8 / 26.0.3 / 32.0.8 / 35.0.7. DigitalOcean blocked an anomalous administrator session on August 16; the investigation found the chain used against Standard edition 16.1.9. Patchstack sees scanning from six IP addresses across Europe, Africa and the US, and a public proof-of-concept against the free edition exists.

CVSS is not asserted here: BleepingComputer and Patchstack state no score, while a search summary gives 9.8 for CVE-2026-15981 and 8.1 for CVE-2026-61979 — reproduced “as reported” in the CVE table below with that caveat. Third-party databases surfaced two other CVE identifiers against the same plugin; only the pair named in the exploitation reporting is published. Neither is KEV-listed and neither carries a federal deadline.
