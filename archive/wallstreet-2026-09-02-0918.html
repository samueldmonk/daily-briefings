The Lead

August private payrolls miss, and two live blogs disagree on which way futures point

The number arrived before the bell and it was soft. ADP reports that private U.S. employers added 38,000 jobs in August, fewer than the 47,000 economists expected and down from July’s upwardly revised 46,000. It is the smallest monthly gain since January. The composition is narrower than the headline: education and health services added 45,000 and led every category, leisure and hospitality added 16,000 and construction 12,000 — while manufacturing lost 17,000, professional and business services shed 16,000, and both natural resources and mining and trade, transportation and utilities fell 5,000 apiece. Almost all of it came from the top of the size distribution: firms with 500 or more employees added 34,000, those with fewer than 50 just 3,000. Friday’s Labor Department report is the number this one is being read as a preview of.

On the clock, precisely. The print reached TheStreet’s live blog between 8:15 and 8:26 AM ET — the page’s per-entry bylines sit between the blocks they date, so which of the two belongs to the ADP entry is not determinable from the page, and the article’s own last-modified metadata reads 8:26 AM ET. A range is published because a range is what the source supports. Worth noting either way: the previous edition of this page researched from 8:35 to 8:47 AM and did not carry it, which is recorded here rather than quietly fixed.

A correction to this page’s own preview. The previous edition’s On the Radar item previewed ADP as “consensus +46,000 against +44,000 previously.” That was wrong in a specific and instructive way: 46,000 is July’s revised prior, not the consensus — the consensus was 47,000, and 44,000 was July as ADP first reported it. A revision had been mistaken for a forecast. The bullet has been replaced with the actual print.

The futures picture changed character between reads, and this page is not going to pretend it didn’t. TheStreet’s live blog, in its 8:26 AM ET update, still opens “stock futures were falling.” Yahoo Finance’s Wednesday live blog gives numbers that say something different: Dow futures up 0.2%, S&P 500 futures edging higher, Nasdaq-100 contracts down 0.1% — which is mixed, not falling. No timestamp was attached to that read in what this desk fetched, so it is printed as a same-morning read and not as a later one. Both are printed with their attributions and neither is adopted as the state of the tape.

A refusal, and the reason for it. A futures quote board consulted this run returned Dow mini 52,900.00 (+340.00), S&P mini 7,647.50 (+51.50) and Nasdaq-100 mini 29,127.50 (+385.50). Those magnitudes contradict every characterisation fetched this run — a +385-point Nasdaq-100 future is not “down 0.1%” and is not “falling” — and nothing fetched establishes the board’s own clock or its change basis. Unreconcilable is not the same as wrong, but it is enough to withhold. No level from that board is published.

A smaller finding, recorded because it is a hazard for anyone citing a live blog: the headline is a moving quantity too. Two fetches of TheStreet’s Sept 2 page minutes apart returned “S&P 500 futures fall as oil prices rise” and “Nasdaq futures fall as oil…”; two renderings of what appears to be the same Yahoo piece — sharing an 082624 identifier prefix — read “futures mixed” on Yahoo and “futures fall” on a syndication partner. A headline cited without a timestamp is a quotation of something that no longer exists.

The driver underneath all of it is unchanged from Tuesday and louder overnight. Iran’s Revolutionary Guards said Wednesday that two oil tankers struck naval mines attempting to transit the Strait of Hormuz, disabling the vessels and forcing their crews off after the ships ignored warnings about an “illegal route.” Iran targeted Jordan, the United Arab Emirates and Kuwait with missiles and drones overnight in retaliation for the latest American airstrikes. President Trump said he was “not trying to force Iran to the bargaining table,” reiterating on Truth Social that the U.S. has “almost total control” over the Strait.

What makes this a bond story rather than only an energy story is the inflation channel. Per The Wall Street Journal, the 10-year Treasury yield rose Tuesday to 4.798%, the highest since January 2025 on LSEG data, and the two-year to 4.369% — what would be its highest settlement in nineteen months — “reflecting rising bets on an interest rate increase by the Federal Reserve in September.” TheStreet, citing CNBC, has the 10-year touching a day high of 4.814% and calls that the highest level since November 2023. Two sources, two different levels and two different superlatives; both are printed here with attribution and neither is reconciled into a single number. One-year-ahead U.S. inflation expectations measured by derivative markets have crept to 2.5% from less than 2% over the past couple of weeks, LSEG data show. A 38,000 payroll print cuts against that pricing; nothing fetched this run says by how much, so nothing is asserted.

The move is not American. The 10-year Japanese government bond yield crossed 3% to a 30-year high, the 10-year German Bund reached 3.364% — unseen since 2011 — the 10-year U.K. gilt rose to 5.255%, its highest since 2008, and the 30-year gilt to levels unseen since 1998. Metzler analyst Leon Ferdinand Bost described global bonds as facing a “perfect storm” of inflation fears driven by higher energy prices, compounded by fiscal concerns and heavy supply at the long end. Daniela Hathorn of Capital.com framed the loop directly: higher oil feeds inflation worries, which feed yields, which strengthen expectations that the Fed “may need to tighten monetary policy further.”
