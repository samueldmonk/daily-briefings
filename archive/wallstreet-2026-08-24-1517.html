The lead

Under an hour from the close, the tape holds its shape — and a sixth cached page nearly got through

As of roughly 3:05 p.m. ET. With under an hour of regular trading left, the session looks as it has looked since the opening bell: blue chips up, semiconductors down, and nothing resolved until Nvidia reports on Wednesday. The freshest index readings this desk could attribute this run put the Dow up about 0.28%, the S&P 500 off about 0.21% and the Nasdaq Composite off about 0.58% on a read stamped roughly 2:24 p.m. ET, with a separate afternoon summary the same run at S&P 500 −0.3%, Nasdaq −0.6%, Dow +0.2%. The two agree on direction and disagree only on the width of the technology underperformance, so the range rather than a single figure is what is asserted here. No reading on this page is offered as the live tape, because every market page fetched this run was serving cached quotes — and one of them nearly got through, which is the story of this edition. Yahoo Finance served this desk a stale page for the sixth time today, and this was the most convincing one yet: its market strip carried the S&P 500 at 7,659.82 (−14.55, −0.19%), the Dow at 53,392.55 (+115.54, +0.22%) and the Nasdaq Composite at 26,067.27 (−113.18, −0.43%), and every one of those reconciles to the cent against Friday’s verified closes of 7,674.37, 53,277.01 and 26,180.46. Its crude and gold quotes back out to the identical prior closes this desk established earlier in the session — $87.06 for the WTI October contract, $4,680.60 for the gold futures contract. On arithmetic alone the page passes every internal test this desk applies. What caught it was the clock: the strip’s own countdown read “U.S. markets close in 3h 1m,” and three hours and one minute before a 4 p.m. close is 12:59 p.m. ET — roughly two hours before this edition was built, and older than the readings already published here at the previous edition. The lesson is the one already written down in this desk’s standing notes, in its sharpest form yet: reconciling against Friday proves a figure is real, not that it is current, and a perfectly self-consistent quote board can still be two hours old. The countdown check is the only thing that separated the two, and it has now caught Yahoo six times in one session. Those quotes are used below only where they are explicitly stamped 12:59 p.m. ET and only where they improve on an undated earlier read. The cause of the chip weakness is unchanged. 24/7 Wall St. reported at 11:29 a.m. ET that memory stocks slid on weekend reports Washington may permit Apple to source DRAM from China’s ChangXin Memory Technologies (CXMT) and NAND flash from Yangtze Memory Technologies (YMTC), described as a possible diplomatic gesture ahead of President Xi Jinping’s planned US visit, expected on or around September 24. The tape traded it as if one had: SanDisk down 9%, Micron down 7%, Western Digital down 7% and SK Hynix down 5% in that 11:29 a.m. read, the uniformity across NAND and DRAM names the tell that this is a policy-headline shock rather than a fundamental re-rating. The session’s biggest identified single-name decliner remains Applied Optoelectronics, down 11% this morning on The Motley Fool’s read, after the company announced a $600 million equity offering.

The event risk has come off the podium. Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent held his press conference at the Treasury Building at 1 p.m. ET and announced Operation Economic Outcast — in his words “an unprecedented campaign against the Islamic Republic of Iran and its enablers,” begun at President Trump’s direction. Treasury’s Office of Foreign Assets Control is sanctioning more than 60 entities, individuals and vessels accused of helping Iran procure nuclear and missile technology, run cyber operations or generate oil revenue, and the package widens secondary-sanctions exposure across what Bessent called “five of Iran’s most vital lifelines that it exploits in other countries — digital assets, technology, gold, aviation, and shipping.” The stated aim is unchanged: pressure Tehran to reopen the Strait of Hormuz and end a war the administration originally said would last four to six weeks and which is now in its sixth month. Bessent trailed the package in a Financial Times op-ed as “an economic D-Day — the single greatest financial offensive ever marshaled against an adversary,” and criticised China last week for historically buying about 90% of Iran’s oil. CNBC reported ahead of the conference that Iran is warning of ship seizures in the Strait of Hormuz. The second, quieter fight is over the long end of the curve: 24/7 Wall St. published at 11:37 a.m. ET that Treasury officials indicated the department is considering drawing on its Treasury General Account — which was approaching $1 trillion — to fund expanded buybacks, and argued the usable buffer is realistically $100 billion to $200 billion rather than the headline figure. The 10-year yield was down four basis points at 4.69% on The Motley Fool’s 11:37 a.m. read, but the pattern of the past week is that relief does not stick.

The damage was done overnight. Asian stocks sank as Samsung Electronics and Alibaba led a technology rout, with the collapse of US–Canada trade talks adding to a broad risk-off tone. Samsung fell after a record shareholder-return plan — 2026 returns of up to ₩110tn — landed short of high expectations, with analysts fixed on the missing buyback and treasury-share-cancellation timetable; the declines reported for the session in accounts fetched this run range from 5.2% to 9%, so no single figure is asserted here. SK hynix went the other way, up 2.4% on a separate ₩40tn repurchase-and-cancellation plan. Micron was down roughly 3% in US pre-market trading as the Asian memory complex sold off. Japan’s Nikkei 225 slipped 0.10% and South Korea’s Kospi dropped 0.71%, while the small-cap Kosdaq gained 0.94% and Australia’s S&P/ASX 200 rose 0.33%. Europe’s Stoxx 600 opened 0.1% lower, technology off 0.78%, with travel, miners and food and beverage each up about 0.4%.

Two policy shocks landed over the weekend. Trade talks between the United States and Canada collapsed late Friday, and additional 50% duties on a slate of Canadian goods took effect at the stroke of midnight under Section 338 of the Tariff Act of 1930 — covering alcohol, hockey equipment, cement, dairy, building materials and certain kinds of clothing. Canadian Prime Minister Mark Carney called the move “a miscalculation,” blamed the breakdown on “uneconomic” and “unfair” US demands, and said retaliatory tariffs will take effect on September 8 targeting dairy, appliances, agricultural equipment, pulp and paper, and electronics. “You’re at war when you get attacked. We got attacked,” he said at a Saturday press conference. The Canadian dollar dipped; the US dollar added 0.2% against it to 1.3790.

The second is Iran. Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent, who is scheduled to hold a press conference Monday afternoon, wrote in a Financial Times op-ed over the weekend that the coming measures will constitute “an economic D-Day — the single greatest financial offensive ever marshalled against an adversary.” Share markets slipped in Asia and oil prices eased as investors waited for the detail.

Underneath it all sits the bond market. TheStreet put the 30-year Treasury yield at roughly 5.28% on Monday, describing it as having climbed above 5.3% to its highest level in nearly two decades, with government bond yields in Japan, France and Germany also at multi-year highs; Bloomberg framed the same move as investor angst over surging government spending, a flood of long-dated bond sales and inflation stuck above the Fed’s target for five years. The long end is backing off: on CNBC’s midday read the 10-year was down four basis points at 4.7% and the 30-year down four basis points at 5.23% after the General Account report. Earlier Monday, Trading Economics had the 10-year at 4.72%, down two basis points, with a second reading of 4.71%, and Investing.com quotes put the 30-year at 5.248%, the five-year at 4.406% and the three-month bill at 3.811%. The Treasury’s response to the selloff — at least doubling the maximum size of its long-term debt buybacks from $2 billion to at least $4 billion for purchases running September 9 through November — had bought only temporary relief until Monday’s follow-up report. Against that backdrop the US Composite PMI reads 56, with services at 56.8 and manufacturing at 53.2. The week’s two big tests arrive together on Wednesday — July PCE in the morning and Nvidia’s fiscal Q2 after the bell, where FXStreet notes more than $92.05 billion of quarterly revenue is expected — before Fed Chair Kevin Warsh delivers his first Jackson Hole keynote on Friday.

Iran sanctionsAnnounced 1 p.m. ET

Bessent launches “Operation Economic Outcast”

Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent used his 1 p.m. ET press conference at the Treasury Building to announce the campaign by name. “Today, at President Trump’s direction, the United States Treasury has begun Operation Economic Outcast, an unprecedented campaign against the Islamic Republic of Iran and its enablers,” he said. Treasury’s Office of Foreign Assets Control is sanctioning more than 60 entities, individuals and vessels accused of helping Iran procure nuclear and missile technology, conduct cyber operations or generate oil revenue. Bessent said the department had mapped the networks Iran uses to smuggle oil and evade sanctions — exchange houses, free-trade zones, banks, shipping registries and aviation links — and that the new measures “broaden secondary sanctions risk for anyone foolish enough to continue conducting business with this regime.” He gave countries still trading with Tehran a brief window to cut those relationships: “If people do not want to meet our expectations than we expect, and they should expect that they will leave the dollar system.” He added that President Trump was telephoning world leaders with specific requests to stop trading with the regime, and that “any entity that facilitates money laundering on behalf of Iran will be removed from the U.S. dollar system.” His summary of the intent: “This is economic asphyxiation of this regime.” CBS News headlined the remarks “The clock just started ticking.”

10Y −4bp$1T TGA

The Treasury’s $1 trillion cash pile becomes the day’s market driver

CNBC reported Monday, citing two senior Treasury officials, that the department could tap its near-$1 trillion Treasury General Account — the government’s cash balance held at the Fed — to help fund the ramped-up bond purchases announced last week. The officials did not say how much of the account would be used. Treasury surprised markets last week by at least doubling the maximum size of its buybacks of off-the-run long-end securities from $2 billion to at least $4 billion, running from September 9 through November, with Secretary Scott Bessent telling CNBC the operations could be larger still. Yields fell on Monday’s report: the 10-year note yield was last down four basis points at 4.7% and the 30-year down four basis points at 5.23%, after the 30-year hit levels last week not seen since 2007. Stock futures and then the cash indexes pared losses alongside the move. The next test is Friday, when Fed Chair Kevin Warsh gives his first Jackson Hole keynote and may be pressed on how he sees the Fed’s relationship with the Treasury and the bond market.

Refreshed at this edition. 24/7 Wall St., publishing at 11:37 a.m. ET, puts a sharper edge on the arithmetic: much of the General Account is already spoken for, and the realistically usable buffer is more like $100 billion to $200 billion — close to the scale Treasury itself modelled in 2025, when its own analysis showed even a $120 billion annual buyback programme would move the roughly six-year weighted-average maturity of marketable debt by only a few weeks. The recent track record supports the scepticism. The 30-year yield fell nearly 10 basis points on August 19 to 5.187%, after touching 5.337% the day before — its highest since 2007 — and the 10-year fell to 4.651%; by August 20 the 10-year had climbed 4.7 basis points back to 4.70% and the 30-year had returned to 5.247%. The buyback expansion itself runs from September 9 through the November 4 quarterly refunding and targets older, less-liquid off-the-run securities for market functioning, not debt reduction. Treasury had already raised long-end buyback frequency from two operations a quarter to four in 2025, lifting the maximum liquidity-support programme from $30 billion to $38 billion per quarter. The framing 24/7 lands on: this is liquidity support, not quantitative easing, and not a fix for the fiscal arithmetic underneath it.

SNDK −9%Apple / CXMT / YMTC

Washington may let Apple buy Chinese memory — and the whole complex gapped down on it

Weekend reports that the Trump administration may permit Apple to source DRAM from CXMT and NAND flash from YMTC hit every name in the memory group at once. 24/7 Wall St., publishing at 11:29 a.m. ET, had SanDisk down 9% to $1,458.29, Micron down 7% to $897.86, Western Digital down 7% to $429.49, SK Hynix down 5% to $154.48 and the Roundhill Memory ETF down 7% to $53.62. Apple has said it is “evaluating all options” on memory supply and that Chinese sourcing “could help us on the supply side and perhaps the pricing side.”

The size of the reaction owes something to how far these names had already run: SanDisk was up 572% year to date through Friday’s close and Micron up 239%. KC Rajkumar of Lynx Equity Research called Monday’s selloff “an overreaction” in a note, writing that his channel checks found CXMT qualified for only one low-volume Mac product and not qualified for iPhones at all, with poor yield on the high-density lpDDR5x DRAM Apple requires, and that Apple has not qualified YMTC’s NAND for any product. “CXMT supply is unlikely to dent the shortage Apple is facing in DRAM, nor could CXMT supply improve Apple’s negotiation position at traditional suppliers such as MU,” he wrote. Both CXMT and YMTC remain on the Pentagon’s Section 1260H list of firms with alleged ties to China’s military-industrial base, and on August 17 Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick told The Wall Street Journal that “it’s not great American companies using Chinese memory” — a session in which Micron and SanDisk rose sharply. No policy decision has actually been announced.
