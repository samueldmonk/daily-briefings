Top Story

Cl0p turns CVE-2026-12569 into a supply-chain heist: engineering data stolen from exposed Windchill and FlexPLM servers

The Cl0p ransomware gang is running a data-theft extortion campaign against internet-exposed PTC Windchill and FlexPLM instances — the product-lifecycle-management platforms that hold product designs from first idea to final manufacturing across aerospace, defense, automotive, heavy machinery, retail and medtech companies (BleepingComputer, Jul 24). Attackers chain a pre-authentication information disclosure in the FlexPLM WSDL endpoint with a server-side flaw in the Windchill login servlet to reach unauthenticated remote code execution, then drop hex-named JSP web shells under /Windchill/login/ (The Hacker News).

Once inside, the crew enumerates file systems, stages engineering and design data, and moves to double extortion — with ransom emails sent from randomly compromised accounts to hundreds of users inside a victim organization (BleepingComputer). The underlying bug, CVE-2026-12569 (CVSS 9.3, improper input validation reachable pre-auth), is not new: PTC published fixes in its June advisory (CS473270), webshell attacks were reported by late June, and CISA added it to the Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalog with a federal remediation date of June 28 (PTC/Help Net Security/The Hacker News).

Defender takeaway: if a Windchill or FlexPLM instance is reachable from the internet, assume it has been probed — apply PTC's patches, pull the servers off the open internet, and hunt for JSP web shells under /Windchill/login/ before an extortion email decides the timeline for you.

Threat Actor Spotlight

Cl0p / Clop Ransomware

The gang behind serial mass-exploitation campaigns is at it again — this time against PTC Windchill and FlexPLM via CVE-2026-12569, stealing product designs and engineering data for double extortion rather than encrypting systems. Extortion emails arrive from compromised third-party accounts and are blasted to hundreds of employees at once; targets span manufacturing, automotive, aerospace and retail (BleepingComputer/The Hacker News/gbhackers).

Breaches & Incidents

Origin Energy — breach confirmed Breach New

The Australian energy retailer, which serves 4.8 million customers, confirmed unauthorized access to customer data — names, addresses, phone numbers, dates of birth, account details and partial payment-card or bank digits — reportedly reached through a fired former employee's still-working logon to its customer-management system. The attacker claims data on 2 million customers won't be leaked after a purported private settlement; Origin has notified the AFP, ACSC and privacy regulator (BleepingComputer/SC Media/The Nightly — attacker claims unverified).

Dolphin X — RAT with an AI score sheet New

A newly documented remote access trojan claims an AI-powered profiling feature that scores and ranks infected users so operators can decide which victims to target first — triage-as-a-service for the criminal side (BleepingComputer, Jul 24).

Exploit code drops: AD certs & GitLab New

Two working exploits published Jul 24: one lets a low-privileged Active Directory user obtain a certificate for a domain controller, and researchers at depthfirst released exploit code for a GitLab flaw patched six weeks earlier, on June 10. Patch-lag windows on both just became attack windows (BleepingComputer).

Tata Electronics — 200K files posted Ransomware

Tata Electronics confirmed a June cybersecurity incident after the World Leaks extortion group published more than 200,000 alleged company files — a cache reportedly of 204,341 files totaling about 630GB (Bright Defense).

EY — third-party platform breach Breach

Ernst & Young disclosed via a Vermont AG filing (Jul 16) that a third-party IT support platform was accessed between Mar 28 and Apr 12, exposing SSNs and financial account codes; affected people get 24 months of Experian monitoring. No ransomware reported.

SonicWall SMA1000 exploitation Exploited

CVE-2026-15409 (CVSS 10.0 SSRF) and companion CVE-2026-15410 are being exploited against SMA1000 appliances; both were added to CISA's KEV with a Jul 17 federal deadline — now more than a week past (SecurityWeek/CISA).
