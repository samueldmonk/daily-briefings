The Lead

Labor Day: no session — and CENTCOM confirms it struck three Iranian tankers as Hormuz traffic hits a four-month low

Markets are closed. Today is Monday 7 September, Labor Day: the New York Stock Exchange and Nasdaq are shut all day, and the U.S. bond market is closed too per SIFMA. The NYSE reopens at normal hours, 9:30 AM ET on Tuesday 8 September. Newly sourced this edition, and it matters to anyone watching a futures screen this afternoon rather than an equity one: the derivatives market is not open all day either. CME Group’s equity index futures halt at 12:00 p.m. Central — 1:00 PM ET — and move to a pre-open state in which order entry, modification and cancellation are permitted but no orders are matched; they reopen at 5:00 p.m. CT. CME also says it will publish no Monday settlement prices for CME, CBOT, NYMEX or COMEX products, and that day orders entered after the Sunday pre-open are for trade date Tuesday 8 September. So the live ticker at the top of this page has roughly two hours of matched trading left in it as this edition publishes, and then a quiet screen that is not the same thing as a flat market. Two returns in the 11:17 edition agree on the 12:00 CT halt; both also carry the standard warning to check the exchange and broker schedule directly, and this desk repeats it rather than presenting a relayed time as authoritative. Everything below on equities reflects Friday 4 September, the most recent completed session. Refreshed this edition: a Trading Economics commodity return dated today has Brent rising toward $97 a barrel on Monday, extending last week’s gains as the United States and Iran exchange strikes, and adds Tehran’s answer to Saturday’s tanker strikes — attacks on tankers and other vessels linked to the United States, and a “restricted” maritime zone beyond the Strait of Hormuz said to be coming in the next few days. “Toward $97” is the direction and the neighbourhood as the return states them; this desk still declines to assert a single Brent print as the live level, for the twenty-sixth consecutive edition, because no timestamped quote was reached. Nor is any VIX level published — twenty-sixth consecutive edition — and on a closed session there would be no live one to publish. Crude is the exception, and it is why this page has led somewhere different from the equity story in each of today’s editions. Oil trades while the NYSE is dark, and over the weekend the U.S.–Iran confrontation moved from missiles to tankers: Brent has run to a six-week high, its highest since 24 July, a date sourced in the 9:17 edition from EnergyNow quoting Reuters, and — per Trading Economics, fetched directly in the 8:47 edition — nearly 40% above levels from before the outbreak of the war in Iran. Four things were newly sourced in the 9:17 edition, all from EnergyNow’s 7 September market report, fetched directly. U.S. Central Command is now the named source for the strikes — it says American forces hit three Iranian oil tankers on Saturday, one of them near Kharg Island, the centre of Iran’s crude-export system. The physical effect is measurable: Kpler counts an average of only ten commodity ships a day transiting Hormuz over the past ten days, the lowest level since May. The range of outcomes has been priced by a bank willing to put its name to it: Goldman Sachs says crude could reach $120 a barrel if attacks on Middle Eastern shipping intensify, while a normalisation of regional exports could pull it back toward $80. And the producer group offered no relief — OPEC+ left its October output policy unchanged over the weekend while members work toward new quotas. The escalation, the numbers and what is holding the move back are set out under Rates, Bonds & Commodities; the equities story below is unchanged because there was no session in which to change it. The payrolls beat pushed the hike case forward, but it is no longer the newest input: on Thursday 3 September, the day before those numbers landed, Fed governor Christopher Waller said the 11 September CPI report would largely determine his vote, and hike odds fell back to roughly even — and then Friday’s payrolls pushed them back up again. Neither input cancels the other: the vote still turns on Friday’s CPI, but the market is no longer split down the middle. That exchange, and the rebound, are set out in full under On the Radar.

Stocks fell and Treasury yields rose after August nonfarm payrolls grew 162,000 — far above the 53,000 economists polled by Dow Jones had expected. The unemployment rate was 4.1%, and average hourly earnings rose 0.3% month-over-month to $37.75, up 3.1% year-over-year. The read pushed traders toward pricing a Fed hike rather than a cut this month, and carried the 2-year yield to its highest level since January 2025. A level for the 2-year is still not published — the direction is sourced, the number is not. Two details sourced in the 8:19 edition put the beat in context: it was the strongest monthly gain since March, and the two prior months were revised up — June from 20,000 to 31,000 and July from a loss of 23,000 to a gain of 21,000, leaving June and July combined 55,000 higher than previously reported. A caution attached to that last figure, because this desk nearly published it as something else: a return in the 8:19 edition described the consensus as “55,000,” which collides with the two-month revision total. The consensus is 53,000 — independently re-confirmed in the 8:19 edition and now verified thirteen times — and 55,000 is the revision, not the forecast. The two numbers are a coincidence, not a correction.

Under the surface the week was close to flat: the S&P 500 rose 0.1%, the Nasdaq rose 0.4% and the Dow fell 0.3% across the five sessions. The bigger move was in energy, where Brent traded in the mid-$90s after renewed U.S.–Iran hostilities: Kuwait’s army reported missile and drone attacks from Iran on 3 September. That refusal was partly resolved in the 8:47 edition, and is resolved outright below: Trading Economics’ own Brent series, fetched directly then, carries a Previous value of $96.28 against Monday’s print — and with Saturday and Sunday absent from the series, the value before a Monday quote is Friday’s. That is the same $96.28 CNBC reported as the Friday settle, from an unrelated source. Two independent routes to one number is corroboration, so $96.28 is now printed for Friday — still attributed, still not called an official settle, because a CFD series and a settlement price are not the same object. The lower “near $95” returns are superseded rather than reconciled. Shipping through the Strait of Hormuz has thinned even as volumes hold: Energy Secretary Chris Wright told CNBC that more than 17 million barrels transited Hormuz on Monday under U.S. naval protection, a wartime record, while commodity-vessel counts fell to six on Wednesday from eleven on Tuesday. Where that leaves the index. Real Investment Advice puts the S&P 500 at roughly 1% below its record, 1.8% above its rising 50-day moving average and 8.2% above its 200-day — its phrasing is that nothing looks broken on the surface while the momentum picture turns underneath. That is one firm’s reading of the setup, printed with its name on it and not as a forecast. No 10-day average is published: returns in an earlier edition gave two different baselines for that same statistic, so it is withheld.
