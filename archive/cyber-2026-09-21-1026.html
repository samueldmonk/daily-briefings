Top Story

ESET names SparroWocky: a new China-aligned backdoor built for Latin American governments

ESET has identified a fresh cyberespionage campaign by the China-aligned FamousSparrow group using a new backdoor it calls SparroWocky, deployed against government organisations across Latin America. According to ESET telemetry, around 90% of the group’s targets recorded from mid-2025 into 2026 were in Latin America, with deployments observed against government entities in Argentina, Ecuador, Guatemala, Honduras, Panama, Peru, Puerto Rico and Venezuela.

SparroWocky is a modular C++ implant that collects hostnames, usernames, domain names, Windows versions and IP addresses, takes screenshots, executes commands, exfiltrates files over TLS with RC4 encryption and can act as a TCP proxy. It persists through services or registry Run keys, uses a COFF Loader to run Beacon Object Files directly in memory, and borrows SilentMoonwalk to forge call stacks so monitored operations are harder to trace.

Delivery runs through a trident loader scheme that relies on DLL sideloading: a legitimate executable loads a modified DLL, which pulls the encrypted payload and configuration from a .dat file. The decrypted PE has its MZ and PE magic values stripped before being reflectively loaded — ESET believes this is meant to defeat defences that pattern-match for suspicious code in memory.

The name comes from the opening stanza of Lewis Carroll’s Jabberwocky, found inside early samples; the strings appear to come from test vectors shipped with the Mbed TLS library the backdoor uses for secure communications. It has largely replaced the group’s older SparrowDoor implant. FamousSparrow has been active since at least 2019, was first known for targeting hotels, and was publicly documented by ESET in 2021 after it was seen exploiting ProxyLogon. ESET notes one targeted Panamanian organisation is involved in a commercial dispute over two ports in the Panama Canal area, and suspects the operation may have been intended to obtain advance notice of local government decisions — while acknowledging the reason for the regional focus remains unclear.

Patch Priority

Due today: CVE-2025-39682, Linux kernel (kTLS receive path) — federal deadline 21 September

CISA added CVE-2025-39682 to the Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalog on 18 September with federal remediation required by 21 September under Binding Operational Directive 26-04 — that is today, 0 days left. The flaw is an improper check for an unusual condition in net/tls/tls_sw.c: when an application sets TCP_ULP to “tls” and the kernel takes ownership of decrypting incoming TLS records, a zero-length record retrieved from the rx_list bypasses the intended recvmsg() record-type handling. Two sibling Linux kernel flaws were added on the same date and carry the same deadline — only CVE-2025-39682 was named by identifier in sources read this run, the other two being carried from this desk’s standing record — and CISA has marked all three as requiring forensic triage — agencies must investigate potentially exposed assets for evidence of compromise rather than treat patching as the whole response.

Threat Actor Spotlight

ShinyHunters

The extortion crew turns up twice in this briefing. It claimed and then partly documented the intrusion into Florida’s DAVID driver and vehicle database, publishing a proof archive and later supplying Hackread.com with an account of its access that FLHSMV’s own statement only partly matches. Separately, it hacked and defaced the leak site of the Clop ransomware operation — a reminder that the extortion ecosystem preys on itself as readily as on its victims. ShinyHunters has told reporters its Florida access began with credentials from an infostealer log, not a password-reset exploit; it says it used weaknesses in the portal’s password-recovery process only to widen access afterwards.

Breaches & Incidents

GovernmentConfirmed

Florida DMV confirms the DAVID breach

The Florida Department of Highway Safety and Motor Vehicles says it learned of the breach on 4 September 2026 and traced it to credentials belonging to a single Plant City Police Department user that were “improperly housed” on that employee’s personal electronic device. The agency says the incident was contained. Hackread.com’s analysis of the leaked material puts the compressed archive at about 52.3 GB across six folders holding 612,982 ZIP files — explicitly not a count of affected people, since multiple archives can relate to the same person or transaction. Files reviewed include front and back scans of Social Security cards, US and international driving licences, residence documents and employment authorization cards, many stored as directly viewable, unredacted scans. FLHSMV has filed the required breach notice with the Florida Attorney General under §501.171 and has not said how many residents are affected.

NewIdentity

IDScan.net confirms a breach behind a 153-million-licence leak

The Louisiana-based ID-verification firm — which processes checks for car rental companies, retailers and cannabis dispensaries — confirmed that attackers accessed customer data on its cloud platform. It says it received information on or around 1 September 2026 that data may have been accessed without authorisation, and posted a notice on 4 September. The dark-web collection linked to it holds more than 153 million driver’s licences, 10 million ID cards, 3 million travel documents and 579,000 medical cards including marijuana dispensary cards. Exposed fields may include full names, driver’s licence numbers and numbers from other government-issued identification. The FBI’s New Orleans field office has opened a formal investigation.
