The Lead

Stocks fade into the close after Warsh says inflation's underlying trend has not improved — but the week still ends green

The S&P 500 fell 0.25% to 7,711.76 on Friday, the Nasdaq Composite slid 0.52% to 26,402.42, and the Dow Jones Industrial Average finished essentially flat — down 9.45 points, or 0.02%, to 53,559.99. These are the official closing figures for Friday, August 28, and the three of them reconcile against Thursday's close: the Dow's 9.45-point loss puts Thursday at 53,569.44, which is exactly the level Thursday's recap carried, and Friday's Nasdaq close sits 0.5234% below Thursday's, which rounds to the reported 0.52%.

The move followed Fed Chair Kevin Warsh's address at the Kansas City Fed's annual symposium in Jackson Hole, where he said: “While this summer's PCE and CPI readings were better than expected, they do not tell me that underlying trends have meaningfully improved.” Semiconductors carried the decline — Nvidia gave back more than 3% a day after jumping 8.7% on its earnings, and Intel also fell.

The tape faded through the afternoon, and this page can say so on a like-for-like basis. The Motley Fool's midday report on the same session was headlined “Stocks Edge Higher”; the session ended lower on the S&P 500 and Nasdaq. Midday and close are two reads of one session, which is what makes the comparison legitimate — but no intraday percentage is asserted here, because none was stated with a clock attached.

The week finished higher regardless: the S&P 500 rose 0.5%, the Nasdaq 0.9%, and the Dow 0.5% — the Dow's first winning week in three.

On the Radar

  • The September FOMC decision. A majority of fed funds futures traders expect the committee to hold at 3.50%–3.75%, with roughly a 65% probability priced for no change. Warsh's Jackson Hole framing — that better PCE and CPI prints do not yet signal an improved underlying trend — is the case for patience rather than easing.
  • Nvidia's guidance sets the bar for the AI trade. Management forecast fiscal 2027 third-quarter revenue of $108 billion, plus or minus 2%, above the roughly $106 billion Wall Street expected and representing more than 89% year-over-year growth. The stock gave back more than 3% on Friday anyway, which is the more interesting datapoint.
  • Seasonality and the midterms. August and September have historically been seasonally weaker months for equities, and uncertainty may increase as the midterm elections approach — though the same commentary frames periods of weakness as opportunities to add.
