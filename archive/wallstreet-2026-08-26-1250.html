Meta writes a $16.7 billion cheque, and it is still the news of the morning

The session’s lead is a legal settlement, not a data point. Meta and a coalition of state attorneys general have settled the federal trial over allegations that the company misrepresented the extent of child-related mental-health harms caused by Facebook and Instagram. CNBC puts the figure at $16.7 billion; Bloomberg’s headline says Meta agreed to pay “up to” $16.7 billion; NBC News frames it as “up to $16 billion”; and a separate read this run gives $16.68 billion. ⚠ Those are four different renderings of one number and they are published unmerged — nothing here is averaged or rounded into a single figure. The case was brought by 29 states and co-led by California Attorney General Rob Bonta with the attorneys general of Colorado, New Jersey and Kentucky; California could receive $1.5 billion to $2.1 billion if the court approves. The deal requires new guardrails — limits on how long young users can scroll, and a bar on switching off certain safety settings without parental consent. The filing states that Meta “denies the allegations against it and that it has any liability to the Plaintiffs,” and a judge still has to sign off.

U.S. markets are open, and roughly an hour into the session. The first of Wednesday’s two set-piece events is behind us with the number confirmed; the second does not arrive until after the close. The Commerce Department reported on Wednesday that the personal consumption expenditures price index rose 0.2% from a month ago and was up 3.7% on an annual basis in July. Both figures were hotter than the expectations of economists polled by LSEG, who projected readings of 0.1% and 3.6% respectively. Core PCE, which excludes food and energy, was up 0.2% on a monthly basis and is 3.3% higher than last year — both in line with the LSEG poll. That is Fox Business, published 8:35 a.m. EDT today under Eric Revell’s byline and fetched in full; CNBC independently headlines its own release story “Fed’s preferred inflation gauge shows core prices rose 3.3% annually in July.”

What the split actually says. The core rate did what the market was positioned for — it held. At 3.3% it sits below May’s 3.4% year-on-year peak but still comfortably above the Fed’s 2% target, and on one preview’s count a hold at that level marks the 65th consecutive month above target. The surprise is on the headline, where energy does its work: 3.7% against a 3.6% forecast, and a monthly 0.2% against 0.1%. It is a tenth in each direction — not a regime change, but not the clean disinflation print the tape wanted either.

And the direction of the risk is still up. Ahead of the release, the CME FedWatch Tool put a 38% probability on a quarter-point rate hike in September — down from 55% one month ago, per FXStreet, which attributes the decline to doubts about Chairman Kevin Warsh’s commitment to fighting inflation in the absence of forward guidance. Read that number carefully: this is a market pricing the odds of a hike, not a cut. Bloomberg reported Tuesday that falling oil prices were curbing expectations for more than one Federal Reserve interest-rate hike in the coming year — the debate is over how many, not whether. Strategists at DBS Bank frame Friday’s Jackson Hole appearance as a test of whether Warsh can spell out “how a Fed without forward guidance intends to anchor expectations, how much tightening the Fed is prepared to tolerate through long-term yields, and the policy boundary between the Fed and the Treasury.” The dollar has not been waiting for an answer: the US Dollar Index is 0.75% lower on the month and more than 2.5% below its late-July top.
