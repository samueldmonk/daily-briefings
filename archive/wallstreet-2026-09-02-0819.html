The Lead

Futures point lower into the open as the Iran escalation feeds oil, and oil feeds the bond market (pre-open read, ~5:00 AM ET)

U.S. stock futures edged lower in premarket trading Wednesday “as rising oil prices and bond yields sent ripple effects across markets,” Yahoo Finance reported in a 5:00 AM ET update: Dow futures off 0.1%, S&P 500 futures off 0.2% and Nasdaq-100 contracts down 0.5%, extending Tuesday’s losses. A later read of the same quote board — taken with roughly three hours and forty minutes to the opening bell — had S&P futures at 7,623.00 (−19.75, −0.26%), Dow futures at 52,756.00 (−72.00, −0.14%) and Nasdaq-100 futures at 28,948.00 (−177.50, −0.61%) — a different instrument from the Nasdaq Composite whose close appears in the Weekly Scorecard, with the VIX at 16.77 (+2.63%).

The driver is unchanged from Tuesday and has simply grown louder. Stocks began September unsteadily as an escalation in the war in Iran pushed Brent crude above $95 a barrel; on Tuesday President Trump threatened to hit Iran “much harder” if the country retaliates against a barrage of airstrikes launched that day. Kyle Rodda of Capital.com told TheStreet that markets “will be hopeful” the flare-up is another attempt to “escalate to de-escalate.”

What makes this a bond story rather than only an energy story is the inflation channel. Per The Wall Street Journal, the 10-year Treasury yield rose Tuesday to 4.798%, the highest since January 2025 on LSEG data, and the two-year to 4.369% — what would be its highest settlement in nineteen months — “reflecting rising bets on an interest rate increase by the Federal Reserve in September.” TheStreet, citing CNBC, has the 10-year touching a day high of 4.814% and calls that the highest level since November 2023. Two sources, two different levels and two different superlatives; both are printed here with attribution and neither is reconciled into a single number. One-year-ahead U.S. inflation expectations measured by derivative markets have crept to 2.5% from less than 2% over the past couple of weeks, LSEG data show.

The move is not American. The 10-year Japanese government bond yield crossed 3% to a 30-year high, the 10-year German Bund reached 3.364% — unseen since 2011 — the 10-year U.K. gilt rose to 5.255%, its highest since 2008, and the 30-year gilt to levels unseen since 1998. Metzler analyst Leon Ferdinand Bost described global bonds as facing a “perfect storm” of inflation fears driven by higher energy prices, compounded by fiscal concerns and heavy supply at the long end.
