The Lead

Stocks open mixed after a soft payroll print — and oil gives back its overnight spike (as of the 9:30 open)

The bell has rung, and the tape is not doing the one thing the futures session kept arguing about. Shortly after the 9:30 open, CNBC has the Dow up about 187 points, or 0.4%, the S&P 500 up 0.1% and the Nasdaq Composite down 0.1% — mixed, with the industrials leading and the growth end lagging. Those are the only September 2 index figures on this page, they are moves and not levels, and the only clock attached to them by the source is “shortly after the opening bell.” No more precise timestamp was fetched, so none is asserted.

The reversal worth watching is in crude, not in equities. At 7:04 AM ET TheStreet had WTI +0.32% at $90.51 and Brent +0.57% at $95.19, rising on the overnight strikes. By its 9:24 AM ET update the same live blog has oil falling after touching more than one-month highs earlier in the session: WTI −0.71% at $89.58 and Brent −0.39% at $94.28, as traders weighed supply-disruption risk against evidence, per Reuters, that crude is still reaching the market. This is a genuine intraday turn inside one source, not a disagreement between two — both prints carry the same byline and their own clocks. Saxo Bank’s head of commodity strategy Ole Hansen calls it “a binary risk”: news of a deal “could send prices tumbling, while any escalation would further undermine the prospect of a peace deal,” leaving oil volatile with “a potential $5 move in either direction.”

And a new policy headline landed seventeen minutes before the open. Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick told CNBC that the administration is developing a tariff framework for semiconductors and that companies know the tariffs are coming, confirming a Politico report from last week. “I think what you’re going to see is targeted, thoughtful tariff policy that basically says if you build here, you don’t pay, but if you don’t build here, expect to pay to enter the greatest market in the world,” Lutnick said, adding: “We will be successful in semiconductors. They’re going to be built in America.” No detail of the framework has been published and no chip-stock reaction to it was sourced this run, so none is described — but it arrives on the morning Broadcom reports.

The number arrived before the bell and it was soft. ADP reports that private U.S. employers added 38,000 jobs in August, fewer than the 47,000 economists expected and down from July’s upwardly revised 46,000. It is the smallest monthly gain since January. The composition is narrower than the headline: education and health services added 45,000 and led every category, leisure and hospitality added 16,000 and construction 12,000 — while manufacturing lost 17,000, professional and business services shed 16,000, and both natural resources and mining and trade, transportation and utilities fell 5,000 apiece. Almost all of it came from the top of the size distribution: firms with 500 or more employees added 34,000, those with fewer than 50 just 3,000. Friday’s Labor Department report is the number this one is being read as a preview of.

On the clock, precisely. The print reached TheStreet’s live blog between 8:15 and 8:26 AM ET — the page’s per-entry bylines sit between the blocks they date, so which of the two belongs to the ADP entry is not determinable from the page, and the article’s own last-modified metadata reads 8:26 AM ET. A range is published because a range is what the source supports. Worth noting either way: the 8:48 AM edition of this page researched from 8:35 to 8:47 AM and did not carry it, which is recorded here rather than quietly fixed.

A correction to this page’s own preview. The 8:48 AM edition’s On the Radar item previewed ADP as “consensus +46,000 against +44,000 previously.” That was wrong in a specific and instructive way: 46,000 is July’s revised prior, not the consensus — the consensus was 47,000, and 44,000 was July as ADP first reported it. A revision had been mistaken for a forecast. The bullet has been replaced with the actual print.

The pre-open futures record, kept because it is now settled by the tape rather than by argument. TheStreet’s 8:26 AM ET update opened “stock futures were falling”; Yahoo Finance’s Wednesday live blog gave Dow futures up 0.2%, S&P 500 futures edging higher and Nasdaq-100 contracts down 0.1%, which is mixed. The 9:18 AM edition printed both and adopted neither. The open has now come in mixed, with the Dow leading and the Nasdaq lower — which resembles the Yahoo read. That is not a retroactive vindication and is not published as one: a futures quote at 8 AM and an index move just after 9:30 are different measurements, and one matching the other is consistent with the earlier read having been right, with it having been wrong, or with coincidence.

A refusal, and the reason for it. A futures quote board consulted for the 9:18 AM edition returned Dow mini 52,900.00 (+340.00), S&P mini 7,647.50 (+51.50) and Nasdaq-100 mini 29,127.50 (+385.50). Those magnitudes contradict every characterisation fetched this run — a +385-point Nasdaq-100 future is not “down 0.1%” and is not “falling” — and nothing fetched establishes the board’s own clock or its change basis. Unreconcilable is not the same as wrong, but it is enough to withhold. No level from that board is published.

A smaller finding, recorded because it is a hazard for anyone citing a live blog: the headline is a moving quantity too. Two fetches of TheStreet’s Sept 2 page minutes apart, made for the 9:18 AM edition, returned “S&P 500 futures fall as oil prices rise” and “Nasdaq futures fall as oil…”; two renderings of what appears to be the same Yahoo piece — sharing an 082624 identifier prefix — read “futures mixed” on Yahoo and “futures fall” on a syndication partner. A headline cited without a timestamp is a quotation of something that no longer exists.

The driver underneath all of it is unchanged from Tuesday and louder overnight. Iran’s Revolutionary Guards said Wednesday that two oil tankers struck naval mines attempting to transit the Strait of Hormuz, disabling the vessels and forcing their crews off after the ships ignored warnings about an “illegal route.” Iran targeted Jordan, the United Arab Emirates and Kuwait with missiles and drones overnight in retaliation for the latest American airstrikes. President Trump said he was “not trying to force Iran to the bargaining table,” reiterating on Truth Social that the U.S. has “almost total control” over the Strait.

What makes this a bond story rather than only an energy story is the inflation channel. Per The Wall Street Journal, the 10-year Treasury yield rose Tuesday to 4.798%, the highest since January 2025 on LSEG data, and the two-year to 4.369% — what would be its highest settlement in nineteen months — “reflecting rising bets on an interest rate increase by the Federal Reserve in September.” TheStreet, citing CNBC, has the 10-year touching a day high of 4.814% and calls that the highest level since November 2023. Two sources, two different levels and two different superlatives; both are printed here with attribution and neither is reconciled into a single number. One-year-ahead U.S. inflation expectations measured by derivative markets have crept to 2.5% from less than 2% over the past couple of weeks, LSEG data show. A 38,000 payroll print cuts against that pricing; nothing fetched this run says by how much, so nothing is asserted.

The move is not American. The 10-year Japanese government bond yield crossed 3% to a 30-year high, the 10-year German Bund reached 3.364% — unseen since 2011 — the 10-year U.K. gilt rose to 5.255%, its highest since 2008, and the 30-year gilt to levels unseen since 1998. Metzler analyst Leon Ferdinand Bost described global bonds as facing a “perfect storm” of inflation fears driven by higher energy prices, compounded by fiscal concerns and heavy supply at the long end. Daniela Hathorn of Capital.com framed the loop directly: higher oil feeds inflation worries, which feed yields, which strengthen expectations that the Fed “may need to tighten monetary policy further.”

Those are September 1 movers. The Apple percentage was worse than stale: the Schwab page says only that Apple “stood its ground early” and is up almost 17% year to date, and carries no daily percentage at all.
