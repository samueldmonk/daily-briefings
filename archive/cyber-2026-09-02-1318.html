Top Story

Berlin refuses Rhysida’s ransom — and the group opens an auction three weeks before a state election

Berlin’s state government has confirmed it is dealing with an extortion attempt following an August cyberattack on the city-state’s administrative network, and has already refused the ransom. In a joint statement with Interior Senator Iris Spranger, reported by Reuters on 28 August, officials said Berlin would not submit to extortion.

Rhysida claims it took 5.79 TB from Berlin agencies — contracts, emails, phone numbers, passwords and material described as classified — and is demanding 30 bitcoin, put at close to $2.3 million at the exchange rate cited in reporting. The group set a one-week countdown before it says it will begin publishing, and has threatened to auction the trove.

Officials say election infrastructure was not affected ahead of Berlin’s September 20 state election. The timing is the point: a public refusal to pay, made in the window where a leak does the most political damage, is the clearest recent test of the “don’t negotiate” posture by a major European government.

Patch Priority

CVE-2026-64849 — MLflow SSRF — federal deadline is TODAY, 0 days left

CVSS 9.3. All MLflow versions before 3.15.0. An unauthenticated attacker can make the MLflow Tracking Server issue arbitrary HTTP requests to internal network resources including cloud provider metadata endpoints — the reported outcome is live AWS/GCP/Azure credential theft and account compromise. CISA added it to the KEV catalog on August 19 with a remediation date of September 2, 2026. That is today. Upgrade to 3.15.0 or later.

This box outranks the SonicWall pair deliberately: CVE-2026-83548 carries the higher CVSS and is confirmed exploited, but no federal clock for it was sourced this run, and a deadline that expires today beats a severity score that does not. Patch both.

Threat Actor Spotlight

VishingNew

“Spring Ring” — the IT help desk that calls you on Teams

A coordinated voice-phishing campaign tracked as Spring Ring ran between January and April 2026 and reached more than 150 employees at more than 10 companies across industries. The operators registered external Microsoft Teams tenants named to look internal — “ITProtectionDepartment”, “MandatoryNetworkMonitoring” — on the same onmicrosoft.com format legitimate Microsoft 365 customers use. They first buried a target’s inbox in spam, then messaged over Teams offering to help with the “email bombing”, convinced the victim their machine was infected, and talked them into opening a Windows Quick Assist remote session. From there, harvested credentials were used to pivot over WinRM (TCP 5985) to domain controllers and identity infrastructure — protocol-native traffic that blends into normal administration. The control that works here is not a signature; it is blocking external Teams tenants from initiating chats, and treating any unsolicited Quick Assist request as an incident.
