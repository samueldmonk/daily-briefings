The Lead

A global bond sell-off does the driving: Japan’s 10-year touches 3% for the first time in 30 years, the U.S. 30-year adds four basis points to 5.251% — and the eighth intraday read sets a new low for the S&P 500 while the Nasdaq Composite has been worse earlier in the day (as of ~2:45 PM ET)

The United States and Iran returned to open hostilities over the weekend. U.S. forces struck an Iranian island in the Strait of Hormuz on Sunday and Tehran retaliated against U.S. military targets in the Middle East; President Trump told a Fox News reporter “We’re going to hit them hard… There will be a response.” After six months of war the conflict remains at an impasse, with Tehran keeping the Strait closed and Washington maintaining a counter-blockade of Iranian ports (AFP via Yahoo Finance). Schwab’s market-open note adds an overnight attack on a cargo ship transiting the Strait, and TheStreet reports that two oil tankers — one Saudi and one South Korean-owned — were hit by projectiles on Monday night as the two sides resumed hostilities. Crude has extended again: TheStreet has U.S. oil up another 4% on Tuesday, trading above $89 a barrel — a higher mark than the $86.57 Trading Economics carried earlier in the session, and a fourth clock on one up-move rather than a fourth claim. Two further supply facts landed this run: a supertanker caught fire in the Strait after striking two naval mines, and refinery strikes in Russia have tightened global refining capacity, driving refined-product margins to fresh highs (Trading Economics).

That pushed the inflation trade back to the front. Futures were already soft before the bell — S&P 500 futures −0.57%, Dow futures −0.53% and Nasdaq-100 futures −1.02% at ~5:00 AM ET (Yahoo Finance) — and the weakness carried into the session. The session now has a shape rather than a single snapshot. Right after the opening bell the S&P 500 was down 0.7% and the Nasdaq Composite 1.3%, with the Dow off 363 points, or 0.7%. By late morning the Dow had recovered to a loss of roughly 0.1%, the S&P 500 was down about 0.3% and the Nasdaq Composite around 0.7% (Yahoo Finance live blog). That bounce has since faded. A separate read has the Dow off 221 points, or 0.4%, with the S&P 500 down 0.3% and the Nasdaq Composite 0.5%; a fourth read, again from Yahoo’s live blog, had the decline widening to 0.5% on the Dow, 0.7% on the S&P 500 and 1.4% on the Nasdaq Composite. A fifth read fetched this run has the Dow down 412 points, or 0.8%, with the S&P 500 off 0.7% and the Nasdaq Composite 0.9% (TheStreet). So the session is a round trip, not a straight line: sold hard at the bell, recovered through the middle of the morning, then given back. ⚠ Note what the fifth clock does to the fourth: the Dow’s loss widened (−0.5% to −0.8%) while the Nasdaq’s narrowed (−1.4% to −0.9%) — the two indices moved in opposite directions between the two reads, which is why an arc with five time-stamped legs is publishable where an averaged “current” number would not be. Every one of those figures is intraday and none is a close; no September 1 index level is published on this page. The reads are printed at their own clocks and none is adopted as the number.

The morning’s two data releases have now landed, and both came in on the soft side of the hawkish case. The ISM Manufacturing PMI registered 54.6% in August, a full percentage point below July’s 55.6% and short of the 55.2 estimate. The sector is still expanding — an eighth consecutive month — but it lost ground on the measures that matter for momentum: New Orders 53.7 (from 56.7), Employment 51.2 (from 52.8) and Production 58.3 (from 58.5). Separately, the July JOLTS report at 10:00 AM ET showed job openings little changed at 7.3 million — quoted at 7.271 million against a 7.300 million estimate — with the openings rate steady at 4.4%, hires and total separations both around 5.1 million, quits at 3.1 million and layoffs at 1.7 million. Job openings rose in durable-goods manufacturing (+76,000) while hires in professional and business services fell (−188,000). The tension is the story: the case for a September hike is being built on oil-driven inflation, not on an economy running hot, and this morning’s prints did nothing to argue the latter.

The bond move is global, and the record it is setting is a duration record, not a level record

The selling is not confined to Treasuries. Japan’s 10-year government bond briefly touched 3% for the first time in 30 years, and U.S. long yields moved with it — the reason the day is being written up as a global bond sell-off rather than a Fed story. ⚠ One widely repeated line about the 30-year needs splitting in two, because two different records are being run together. A read fetched this run says U.S. 30-year bonds “reached levels not seen since 2006.” That is not what the underlying reporting says. Bloomberg’s September 1 piece is headlined on the stretch: the U.S. 30-year enters September on its worst stretch since 2006. CNBC supplies what that stretch is — the 30-year yield has spent 55 days above 5% so far this year, the most in any year since 2006 — while the level record is a different and shorter one: 5.34% in mid-August, the most elevated since 2007, reported at the time as roughly 10 basis points from a 22-year high. So: duration since 2006, level since 2007. The conflated “levels not seen since 2006” formulation is refused on this page, and the two records are printed apart.

NewLeadership

Apple — John Ternus is chief executive as of today

Today is the day it takes effect. John Ternus succeeded Tim Cook as Apple’s chief executive on September 1, 2026, with Cook becoming executive chairman. The handover was announced on April 20, 2026 and approved unanimously by the board after what the company described as a long-term succession process; August 31 was Cook’s last day in the job after 15 years as CEO, a tenure that began in 2011. Ternus joined Apple in 2001 on the product design team, was senior vice president of hardware engineering, and joins the board with the promotion. He inherits AI-related product delays, competitive pressure, supply-chain and trade problems, and an iPhone launch. On the Radar

  • Apple’s succession is now an execution question rather than an announcement. John Ternus took over as chief executive today, with Tim Cook as executive chairman — a transition announced in April and completed on schedule. The near-term test is dated: an iPhone launch under a new CEO, against a backdrop of AI-related product delays and supply-chain and trade pressure. Cook stays on the board as chairman, so this is a handover rather than a departure.
  • The chip trade is the transmission mechanism to watch, not a sector story. Semiconductors led the downside on a day whose driver was a bond move, not an earnings or demand event — the cleanest available evidence that the market is currently repricing the discount rate rather than the numerator. If long yields stabilise, that is where the reversal should show first.
  • Today’s data has landed — and it is no longer a preview. ISM manufacturing for August: 54.6%, versus a 55.2 estimate and 55.6 in July — expansion for an eighth straight month, but slower, with New Orders 53.7 (56.7 prior), Employment 51.2 (52.8 prior) and Production 58.3 (58.5 prior). JOLTS for July, released 10:00 AM ET: job openings little changed at 7.3 million (quoted at 7.271 million against a 7.300 million estimate), openings rate 4.4%, hires and separations both about 5.1 million, quits 3.1 million, layoffs 1.7 million. Still due on the day: S&P Global US manufacturing PMI final August (53.3 expected, 53.2 previously) and construction spending for July (0% expected, −0.1% previously).
  • The yield story is not only a Fed story, and that matters for how long it lasts. Long-term U.S. yields rose last week despite the Treasury’s expanded buyback programme — which suggests the pressure extends beyond expectations for Fed policy. Heavy government borrowing, an elevated term premium and growing competition for capital continue to underpin yields, and TheStreet frames the result as a less comfortable environment for highly valued equities. Read against the session: a hike bet can be unwound at a single meeting; a term-premium bid cannot.
